How to Talk about 9/11

Last September 11, as I was teaching the elements of a short story, the assistant principal knocked on my door and handed me a note which began "DON'T PANIC." Then, it bulleted the details we now all have embedded in our collective memory. As I told my students what had happened, I watched silence take over the room. There was not a giggle, a question, a tear. They were waiting for me to take it back or fix it, and I couldn't imagine how to do that. We turned on the television and watched the events unfold, beginning a slow and difficult dialogue that spanned the entire year. As I am sure many of you experienced, it was always hard to know what to do with students' pain and worry—there were no answers to the questions 9/11 raised.

As this anniversary approaches, I am convinced of several things. As a teacher, this is one of the hardest emotional and social challenges you will experience in your classroom. It is also one of the most important. I don't believe that any of us can forget this tragedy. However, as educators, it is our duty to create a forum to look at the pain and loss and memory of what happened. It is our calling to nurture and listen to the questions, fears, and range of feelings our students face. I think the Sonic Memorial Project creates a wonderful landscape for this work. And make no mistake about it, this work has the power to heal our students and ourselves. I wish you courage and strength as you take on this challenge.

— Kerry Herlihy

Learning outcomes:
Students will:   Evaluate and synthesize data and feelings and express their thoughts orally and in writing
Participate as knowledgeable, reflective, creative members of their community





Lesson 1: Remembering the Day


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

What were you doing on September 11, 2001?
Where did you hear about the attacks?
How did it make you feel?
How does what you feel today compare to what you felt then?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


WORK IN GROUPS

» Have students pair up and share their responses. While they work, make two columns on the board or a piece of chart paper and label them "9/11/01" and "9/11/02."

Have the students report back and share their partners' responses with the class. As they respond, write down the feelings they express in the appropriate columns.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Discuss with students the range of feelings expressed. Ask them to consider how the feelings in the two columns are similar and how they are different. Ask them to predict how those feelings may shift in two, four, or ten years. What feelings do they think will stay the same? Why?


LISTEN/FREE WRITING

» Read Hilary North's poem "How My Life Has Changed" out loud to the students or play the recording of it. Ask students to write for three to five minutes about what they remember or feel after hearing the poem.


CLASS EXERCISE

» Have students share their responses with the class. Ask students what they feel they can no longer do since 9/11.

Write a collective poem titled "I Can No Longer...," having each student contribute one line beginning with those words. As students say their lines, write them on chart paper. It is okay for lines to be similar, but encourage students to put their ideas into their own words.

After the poem is comp3lete, have students read it out loud. It can be read in many different ways. Students can read their own lines; the class can read the whole poem in unison; or students can say their lines whenever they feel comfortable and as many times as they want. Encourage them to read expressively.

Have students practice the reading one or two times. Record the poem and play it back for students.


WORK IN GROUPS

» Ask students to consider what they can still do. In small groups, have students write a parallel poem of at least ten lines called "I Still Can..."

Ask students to share their poems with the class.


FREE WRITING

» To close the lesson, ask students to write a brief letter to Hilary North, the author of "I Can No Longer...," telling her how they felt about her poem or addressing her loss on a personal level.

If there is time and students choose to, they can share their letters with the class before you collect them.

Lesson 2: Remembering the People of 9/11


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:
Think of someone you know who has died. (If you don't know anyone personally, think of someone in your community.)
What do you remember most about them?
If you could use five words to describe them, what would they be? Why?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


WORK IN GROUPS

» After students have heard the clips, have them write for five to seven minutes. They should make sure to record at least two details about each person they heard and consider the following questions:

Did any of the people remind you of someone you know or knew? How?
What do you think was the most difficult part of those clips? Why?
What would you say to any of the people you just heard from?

Discuss students' responses as a class, focusing particularly on the feelings students had listening to those clips.


CONCLUDING THE LESSON

» To close the lesson, ask students to write down three things for which they would like to be remembered. Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection. If there is time, have each student share one thing before you collect the writings. Review and return them with comments at the next class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES:

» Have students read The Bridge of San Luis Rey, the Pulitzer prize-winning novel by Thornton Wilder. This novel offers a great literary approach to discussions of tragedy and loss. It opens in the aftermath of an inexplicable tragedy and follows one monk as he tries to answer the question "why?"
