Civic Ideals and Practices

In today's civic arena, we are required to think critically about point of view, both from a personal and a societal lens. In the case of 9/11, continuous news feeds from established networks were often countered by reports from alternative media outlets and ordinary citizens broadcasting their accounts via the web. This barrage of viewpoints says a great deal about the time and place in which we live.

The Sonic Memorial Project differs from major media accounts of 9/11 by offering multiple first-person perspectives. This project would not be the same without the media production tools that enable today's democracy of expression. These sound artifacts contributed from around the world offer an unusual opportunity to learn about points of view, diversity of viewpoint, and the value of first-person accounts.

Learning outcomes:
Students will:   Differentiate between fact and opinion
Read or listen to and analyze a story for bias
Compare and contrast points of view of a historical and/or personal event





Lesson 1: News Analysis


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

Where do you get information about the world?
How does media shape knowledge of the world, the events of 9/11, and contemporary societies?
Does the balance between fact and opinion depend upon the media outlet presenting the information?
Where can you get the most complete and balanced information?


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Present the news analysis lesson by discussing the points above.

Ask students to tell you where they first heard about 9/11 and write these sources down on the board or on chart paper. Then ask them to call out any additional sources they consulted for information about the event.

Once all the students' responses have been written on the board, ask students to talk about the differences among the news sources they consulted. Which accounts did they trust the most? The least? Why? Why did they seek out certain sources and not others?


LISTEN

» Have students listen to both personal and "official" accounts of 9/11 from the audio archives. Ask them to make two separate lists while listening: one that records the "facts" presented and one that records the speakers' "opinions." For each story, ask them to note whether the account constitutes mostly facts or mostly opinions.


CLOSING THE LESSON

» Have students write down their answers to the following questions:

How do you tell the difference between a fact and an opinion?
If you could only pick a single story to tell about 9/11, which one would you choose? Why?

Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection before collecting their writings. Review and return them with comments at the next class.


Additional Resources

» The September 11 Digital Archive maintains a host of information related to September 11. Among the collections is "September 11th, Screenshots of Online News Sites, September 11, 2001," in which the front pages of online news sites can be viewed as they appeared on that day.

» The Globalvision News Network brings together more than 200 media partners and journalists around the world who have joined forces to give a local voice to news events of global import.

» Here Is New York: A Democracy of Photos is a photo exhibition that arose from the unprecedented documentation of September 11. In a small storefront at 116 Prince Street in New York, artists set up shop with a scanner and a printer and invited anyone with imagery from the disaster to contribute to the collection, which continues to grow to this day.

» Compare the coverage of 9/11 to coverage of the attack on Pearl Harbor from December 7, 1941. (See the websites listed below for possible sources of information.) How do you think America's news coverage has changed since 1941? How has the definition of "patriotism" changed?
  • American RadioWorks, a documentary project of Minnesota Public Radio and NPR News, produces long-form documentaries for public radio. Its recent collaboration with the Center for Documentary Studies at Duke University, ³Days of Infamy," focuses on man-on-the-street perspectives collected shortly after September 11, 2001, and the bombing of Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.
  • The Smoking Gun, Court TV's website featuring historical documents related to a number of crime- and war-related events, has many items about the September 11 and Pearl Harbor attacks. Search on those topics
  • iBiblio.org has an extensive page of links related to the Pearl Harbor attacks. Documents available include transcripts from Congressional hearings and photographic collections.
  • The Spring Hill, Kansas, school website has posted a number of archival documents about World War II, including articles published after the attack at Pearl Harbor.

Lesson 2: Multiple Perspectives


KEY QUESTIONS

» How do multiple perspectives help us understand events?


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Discuss the above question and listen to several of the selected audio clips here or find others in the archive.


WORK IN GROUPS

» Divide the class into small groups and have each group write about one of these stories they heard from a different point of view, preserving some details from the original account.

For example, a personal account might be described from the point of view of another participant in the event, or adapted for presentation by a newscaster.


CLASS PRESENTATION

» Have the groups share their stories and talk about what they did. How did the story change? Was it more or less "accurate"? Why?


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES

» Create a mock newspaper from 9/11 using any variety of media, from paper and glue to Power Point. Write news articles, editorials, and features and illustrate them with drawings, photographs, or other media.
» Visit the Globalvision News Network website and read three different articles on the same news story. In class discussion, consider the following questions: How does a story differ depending on who is reporting it? What influences this? Which version is registered as the "official" version?

Lesson 3: Electronic "Telephone"


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:
How do we receive (and provide) information?
Do you think this information is accurate? Why? Why Not?
What are the roles of memory and individual perspective in the ways we understand and transmit information?


WORK IN GROUPS

» Divide students into groups of four. Ask one student in each group to listen to one of the suggested audio clips here, or choose a story from the archive to use. (The students who are not listening can use the time to write their thoughts about the key questions in their journals.)

Ask each listener to tell the student sitting next to him or her the story that was played (have the students whisper or sit in an area separate from the rest of the group). Have that student write a letter to the next student about the story. The last student will then tell the story to the whole group.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Ask the groups to tell the class how the stories changed and to speculate about how and why this happened.
