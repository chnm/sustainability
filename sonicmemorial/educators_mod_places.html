The Places and Stories of Our Lives

Everyone has a story. Even you. Sometimes it takes a major event to help define a story's beginning, middle, and end. The Sonic Memorial Project's collection is one way to tell a story. What's yours?

Learning outcomes:
Students will:   Analyze the relationships between culture, work, and identity
Read or listen to and interpret historical first-person accounts
Identify and analyze the changes in a neighborhood over time
Define, interpret, and compare culturally significant events
Identify and analyze the elements of a story
Use a real-life story as a starting point for a creative writing project





Lesson 1: Work, Culture, and Identity

WALKING HIGH STEEL: Mohawk Ironworkers at the Twin Towers

Behind every building are the workaday men and women who sometimes risk their lives to create our great architectural wonders. Very often, their stories are left untold. In this section, you'll hear from a distinctive group, the Mohawks, who toiled at the World Trade Center—first to build it and later to recover the dead and injured from its devastated remains. How do their reflections encourage appreciation for their work and the significance of the World Trade Center?


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

How does an individual contribute to history?
How does work shape one's identity and culture?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Before listening to "Walking High Steel," ask students to go to the story page.

Take 10 minutes and have them answer the following questions:
When did Mohawk ironworking begin? How did this happen?
What buildings did the Mohawk ironworkers construct in New York City?
Look at the pictures on the page. What can you infer about the workers?


BRAINSTORM

» Have students share their responses and ask them to brainstorm a list of questions they have about the Mohawk ironworkers. Write the questions on the board.


LISTEN/FREE-WRITING

» Have students listen to "Walking High Steel."
Afterward, ask them to free-write for five minutes, focusing on the following reflective questions:
What did you learn from the program? What surprised you?
What did you already know?
What questions do you still have?


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Discuss the segment for 15 to 20 minutes. This segment could be explored in many ways. Some possible guiding questions include:

In what ways did the Mohawks' work affect their family and culture?
How does work affect your family?
Why do you think people choose the jobs they do?
How do people make decisions about their vocation?
How does a person's choice of vocation affect his or her future?


CLOSING THE LESSON

» To close the lesson, have students write down their answers to the following questions: What would you like to do for work when you get older? Why? Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection, then collect their writings. Review and return them with comments at the next class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITY

» Research industries in the surrounding community. Are there industries that are identified with a specific cultural or ethnic group? Why might those people choose the jobs they do?

Interview someone who has a job of interest to you, then write or record a day in the life of that worker.

Lesson 2: Place and Progress

RADIO ROW: The Neighborhood Before the World Trade Center

New York is an old city. In the memories of its people and in the network of its streets, there are many layers of history. Where the World Trade Center stood there was once a neighborhood called Radio Row. What does its history tell us about memory, place, and what happens when a whole neighborhood is erased from sight?


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

How does the identity of a place change?
Can a place change without losing something?
What can we learn from studying the character of a place?
What is the relationship between a place and its people?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


GALLERY DISCUSSION

» Have a “gallery” discussion:
Post four to six pieces of chart paper, each with a guiding question written on it, around the room. Divide the students into small groups. Ask each group to read and discuss one question for two to three minutes, write their responses for two to three minutes (with one student acting as a recorder) and then rotate to the next question.

Possible guiding questions include:
  • What do you think was lost when Radio Row was demolished? What was gained?
  • What was there before Radio Row? What do you think will be there in the future?
  • If you could add one building to your community, what would it be? Why?
  • If a building were removed from your community, how would the community be different?

When the groups have had the chance to complete every question, choose one person at each question to read all responses back to the class.


CLOSING THE LESSON

» To close the lesson, have students write down their answers to the following questions:

Does a community always have to lose something in order to change and grow? Give an example from your own community or another community you know.

Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection, then collect their writings. Review and return them with comments at the next class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITY

» Create a local “scavenger hunt” to find interesting facts about your neighborhood, its buildings, and its industry. (See “History and Time” for lessons on identifying and collecting artifacts to make time capsules.)

Talk to historians or people who have lived in your community for a long time. What areas of your community have changed over time? How did this change come about? Was it controversial? What was lost? What was gained? Create an audio portrait or write an essay about the changes that have occurred in your community.

Photocopy a map of your neighborhood (often available in the front of your phone book or online at Mapquest), then create a color-keyed legend that might include some of the following: commercial buildings, retail buildings, residential buildings, recreational areas, playgrounds, public spaces, public art, streets, vacant land, multipurpose land, bike trails, basketball courts. Assemble, compare, and contrast the maps with those made by others in the class and post them for viewing and discussion.

If possible, try to find an older map of the same area and color this one in as well. What are the biggest changes? What is the same?


Additional Resources

» Some of these exercises utilize web-based resources that provide additional data, information, or ideas for further study. Here are some links to point you in the right direction.

Mapquest and RandMcNally are two web sites that offer printable maps of varying scales (from a few blocks wide to a whole state or nation). Simply open the main screen and type the name of the city, and state and zoom to the proper scale.

For links and resources on New York's World Trade Center, check out EricDarton.net for the World Trade Center Living Archive, where you'll find many different kinds of references, from mechanical to philosophical.

Lesson 3: The Elements of a Story

THE BUILDING STEWARDESSES: Construction Guides at the WTC

Most of the elements that make the presentation of a real-life story effective—whether it's on the radio, on TV, or in print—are the same as the elements needed for a successful piece of creative writing. In this lesson, students consider a radio story about the WTC building stewardesses in terms of the storytelling techniques used and then apply these ideas to their own writing.


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

What kinds of stories do you like best? Why?
What is the element that’s most likely to capture your interest in a story: the setting, plot, characters? Or a combination?
What makes a good character?
What makes a good plot?


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Discuss these questions with the class, making sure to talk about setting, characterization, and plot and to introduce the concept of the arc of a story (the beginning, middle, end).


LISTEN/FREE WRITE

» Have students listen to "Building Stewardesses."

Ask them to keep the following questions in mind:

Who are the main characters in this story?
Who is your favorite character? Why?
From what point of view is the story told?
What is the setting—the time, the place?
Divide the story into its three basic sections: the beginning (set-up), middle (complications phase), and the end (the resolution).

Afterward, ask students to free-write for five minutes, focusing on those questions.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Discuss the elements of the story with the class, asking them call out their answers to the questions above and then write them on the board.


CLASS PROJECT

» Ask students to pretend they are either a building stewardess or the director of the World Trade Center construction project. Have them do research on the web to prepare a written presentation about the WTC to interest an uninformed audience. If there is time, have several students present their work to the class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITY

» Create a daybook documenting one day in the life of a stewardess.
The stewardesses passed out booklets to people on the street. Create a booklet, complete with photos, to promote the WTC. You may want to look on the site for helpful information. Your booklet should have at least three major thematic sections.

Listen to another story feature on the Sonic Memorial site and use it as the basis for a creative writing exercise.

Many of the people who called in to the Sonic Memorial phone line had been inspired by the event to write poems or songs. Listen to a few of these voicemails, then write your own poem or song and present it to the class.
