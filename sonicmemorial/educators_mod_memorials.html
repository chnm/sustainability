Memorials



We created the Sonic Memorial website so visitors can find something tangible about the World Trade Center—its sounds and stories—within the intangible space of the Internet. We have assembled a memorial without the strictures of height, length, or width: the perfect home for memories of buildings that no longer exist. It is a place where all can mourn, honor, remember, and contribute their stories. It is a place to keep history alive.

Memorials are a way of giving shape to what the people of a certain time think about the past. In them, today gives its accounting of yesterday. The result: Every memorial says as much about the minds of its creators as it does about the past.

What do the memorials of previous generations say about events such as the Civil War, World War I, World War II, and the Vietnam War? How do the differences among these memorials reflect differences among events and different historical perspectives about them? What will memorials to the losses of September 11 say about us?

Learning outcomes:
Students will   Use critical historical inquiry to reconstruct and interpret the past
Develop a perspective about how our personal sense of relatedness to the past can change
Understand their own life experiences as part of the larger human story
Experience personal stories that reflect varying points of view on history
Investigate, interpret, and analyze multiple historical and contemporary viewpoints





Lesson 1: Memorializing Tragedy




KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

Why do individuals or groups construct memorials and monuments to individual or collective tragedies?
How do spontaneous expressions of memory evolve, as they are observed and interpreted by other individuals or groups?
How might spontaneous memorials help survivors of tragedy make meaning of inexplicable loss?
How do spontaneous memorials differ from more intentional and planned efforts to remember a tragedy?
How do aesthetic choices of material, location, lighting, form, and color affect the interpretation of a memorial?


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Begin a discussion with the following quote by Maya Lin
(Newsweek, September 9, 2002)

"The spontaneity after 9-11 was incredibly moving, the shrines around the firehouses and Union Square in Manhattan [above], or anywhere people left tributes. One of the hardest things to see were the missing posters that just sprang up all over the city. Every time you saw them, you were in tears. But if someone tried to re-create those places, it would become a totally different experience. They would lose their power, because what they had was the spontaneity of raw, pure emotion."

Invite students to think about what Lin means by these memorials losing "their power."

Introduce the idea of both spontaneous memorials and more permanent, celebrated memorials. Memory, History, Memorials, created by Facing History and Ourselves, offers examples of spontaneous memorials created in response to the events of 9/11, as well as other tragedies such as the Oklahoma City bombing, Columbine, and the Rwandan genocide.


CLASS EXERCISE

» Contrast the types of memorials on the Memory, History, Memorials site with celebrated place-based memorials, including the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, Lincoln Memorial, African American Civil War Memorial to United States Colored Troops, and the new World War II National Memorial, which is still under construction.

As a class, compare these varieties of memorials. How are they similar or different? What compels people to memorialize tragedy? What happens when spontaneous memorials evolve into more planned, and permanent expressions of memory?


BRAINSTORM

» Within Memory, History, Memorials, the section on spontaneous memorials highlights the story of how a hit-and-run victim was remembered by a community in Massachusetts. Have students visit this site and reflect on this story. Have students seen other similar memorials before? What are they like? What feelings do they evoke?

As a group, have students brainstorm other examples of spontaneous memorials, either from their community, or from recent history. What are some common qualities to these spontaneous expressions of grief? Who do these memorials belong to? Who should be responsible for documenting or protecting them? What might happen when they cease to be spontaneous, and become something more planned and permanent?


WORK IN GROUPS

» The Memory, History, Memorials site includes four examples of how spontaneous memorials developed, and in some cases, were adapted into larger, more permanent memorials. Divide the class into four groups, and have each group focus on one of the examples from the site:

9/11, Rwanda, Columbine, and Oklahoma City. As they examine each case study, have them consider the following questions:
What were the purposes of the initial spontaneous memorials? What event or events was being memorialized? Who created them? Who came to view them? How did these memorials change over time? What caused these changes?


CLASS PRESENTATION

» Using the Memory, History, Memorials site, have students present their small group inquiry to the larger class. As a whole class, revisit the key questions of this exercise, and consider the following questions:

What is lost and what is gained when a spontaneous memorial gives way to a permanent memorial? What should a community do to honor both the spontaneous expressions of grief, as well as the desire to build a more permanent memorial?


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITY

» Have students return to their communities and try locating examples of spontaneous memorials and/or more permanent memorials. Invite students to research the circumstances in which these memorials were initially created. Students may be able to locate this information in local newspapers or historical societies, or through oral histories of people living in the community. Have students attempt to develop a case study of how a community came to terms with either an individual or collective tragedy.

Lesson 2: Make a Memorial




KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

What purpose do memorials serve?
How do aesthetic choices of material, location, lighting, form, and color affect the interpretation of a memorial?
Does the meaning of a memorial change depending upon the observer? How?
Does the meaning of a memorial change through history? Why or why not?


BRAINSTORM

» Ask students to list the memorials in their community, making sure to note both the events they honor and their locations (cemeteries, places of worship, public parks, plazas).

You may choose to have students brainstorm this information from memory. For a more in-depth project, have them do research at a local library or historical society. Ask students to visit as many memorials as possible to experience them firsthand.


RESEARCH

» Review your local memorials, the memorials listed in Lesson 1 above, and online sites about memorials, including:
  Tomb of the Unknowns at Arlington National Cemetery (also known as the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier) was dedicated on March 4, 1921, when Congress approved the burial of an unidentified American soldier from World War I in the plaza of the new Memorial Amphitheater. To the west are the unknowns from World War II, Korea, and Vietnam. Read about these tombs at this site or at the Arlington National Cemetery's official website and discuss the origins of these tombs. Why are they important memorials to these wars? Follow the link about the Vietnam Unknown, later identified as Michael J. Blassie and removed for burial by Blassie's family. What does it mean that his remains were identified? Should the tomb remain vacant? Why or why not?
  The Widows of War Living Memorial website is a place where widows of all armed conflicts can record and share their stories. Inspired by filmmaker Barbara Sonneborn, who lost her young husband in Vietnam, the website provides a place of reconciliation and peace for widows from around the world, in hopes of making us all think differently about war.
  The Oklahoma City bombing at the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in 1995 caused a huge loss of life. The way that community defined their memorial to those killed is illustrative of the process of coming to terms with terror, loss, and diversity in contemporary American society. Several websites provide information and resources:
  • The Oklahoma City National Memorial has a website that provides a tour of the memorial site, explains some of its symbolic concepts, and provides educational materials.
  • The National Organization for Victim Assistance< has a list of victims and a memorial message board, as well as resources for counseling and crisis intervention.
  • CNN.com provides information and reflection on the bombing.


WORK IN GROUPS

» Who or what would students honor with a memorial?
Have students plan a memorial, first choosing the event or person to be honored, then making a rough sketch, a refined drawing, and a model. The models can be made using sculptural materials (such as clay or cardboard) or electronic elements (such as photographs, text, and information from the web).

Ask the students to present an oral presentation that addresses the following questions:

Who or what is being memorialized?
How should people feel when they see the memorial?
Where will it be located?
What will it be made of?
What images or words appear on the memorial? Why?
What do you think people will like most about your memorial?
What do you think people might misunderstand or dislike about it?

Additional Resources



» Facing History and Ourselves (FHAO) is an international educational organization providing professional development programs, services, and resources to middle and high school teachers. FHAO offers these teachers and their students challenging classroom materials that confront the moral questions inherent in a study of not only prejudice, racism, and anti-Semitism but also courage, caring, and compassion. As part of their study of the historical development and lessons of the Holocaust and other examples of collective violence, students and teachers explore the complex relationships between memory, history, and memorials.

FHAO is developing an interactive website focusing exclusively on memorials. This site addresses several questions that have taken on new meaning since 9/11: How do we remember the past? How do we preserve our memories as a legacy for other generations? How can these memorials help prepare us for the future?

Beginning with an analysis of "spontaneous memorials," the website invites teachers and students to interact with a range of print, visual, and audio resources.

Over the next several months, Facing History and Ourselves will be adding sections to the website. These sections offer opportunities for students, teachers, and community members to examine other dimensions of memory, history, and memorials. They include the following topics:

Traditional Monuments
Memorials in Time
Memory in Conflict
Holocaust Memorials
Counter-Memorials
Narrative Museums
Creating Memorials
