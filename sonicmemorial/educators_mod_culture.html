Culture and Identity

Many accounts of 9/11 have focused on the cultural and religious animosity that precipitated the attack on the United States. The effects of this cultural divide are far-reaching, ranging from a recommitment to this nation's pluralism to deep and bitter divides about culture. We all were asked to reflect on our own identities, our cultural beliefs and actions, and our membership in a variety of social, economic, and demographic groups.

The ways we live out our lives from here forward, and the ways we commemorate those who did not survive, are testaments to our culture and the identities we share. The Sonic Memorial Project offers a way to examine identity from multiple perspectives, starting with our own.

When setting up these exercises, make sure to set ground rules that encourage respect and sensitivity—these topics can be very difficult to discuss. Make it clear that the things students come up with in brainstorming sessions do not have to be the personal beliefs of people in the room. The idea is to create an open, safe forum for learning about and analyzing cultural identity and stereotyping.

Learning outcomes:
Students will:   Become aware of their multiple identities and perspectives
Reflect on the impact of stereotypes and design strategies to address them
Discuss and analyze cultural assimilation, accommodation,
and the impact of traditions on thought and action within their own social group





Lesson 1: Looking In


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

How do you see yourself?
How do others see you?
To what groups do you belong? (Some examples might be family, racial or ethnic groups, gender, religious organizations, sexual preference, and athletic teams.)


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


BRAINSTORM

» After students have had a chance to respond in their journals, ask them to brainstorm the different groups to which they belong. Write these different groups on the board, categorizing them if you wish.


FREE-WRITING

» Ask students to choose one of the groups to which they belong and write for five minutes about why they like belonging to that group. What words, feelings, or actions do they associate with that group?

On the reverse side of the paper, ask students to write for five minutes about what is difficult about belonging to that group. Has there ever been a time when other people stereotyped them because they were associated with that group? How did they react? Explain.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Have several of the students share their writings with the class.


BRAINSTORM

» Ask students to brainstorm stereotypes for a particular group, as well as incidents where these stereotypes have been used against people. Ask them where these stereotypes come from. Why do they think they exist?


WORK IN GROUPS

» Divide the students into small groups of three or four. Ask each group to choose one of the scenarios and use role play to explore strategies for confronting these stereotypes in a productive way. Have the groups present their role plays to the class.


CLOSING THE LESSON

» Close the lesson with a reflective writing assignment that students will present as a "ticket to leave." Ask them to write about which of the strategies used in the role plays were most effective and to explain why. Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection, then collect the assignments. Review and return them with comments at the next class.

Lesson 2: Looking Out


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:
How do the groups to which you belong interact?
How do they influence your beliefs and behavior?


CLASS EXERCISE

» Spend a few moments as a class considering the questions and reviewing the lists groups that students created in Lesson 1. Then ask students to write the names of all the groups to which they belong on a large piece of paper, carefully arranging them in such a way that they can draw circles around the names to indicate which groups overlap and which do not. For instance, one's family may be members of a faith organization, such as a church or synagogue. The church circle might enclose the family circle. Students may need to do several drafts of this exercise to accurately portray their multiple group identities.

Have students share their work with a partner and explain their choices.

Collect the diagrams and ask students to stand in a circle. Explain that when you call out a category, all those who feel like a majority of that category should remain in place, while those who feel like a minority should step into the center of the circle and remain there for five seconds. For example, you may say, "Anyone who feels like a minority as a female, step forward." Or, "Anyone who feels like a minority in school or in this community, step forward." Go through at least five categories. Have students suggest categories.

[Note: The operative word is "feels." A student's decision about what constitutes "majority" or "minority" is an important part of this exercise, revealing his/her own experience as a part of a group. Individual students' perspectives may differ from the group's expectations. Some girls may feel like a minority as a female and choose to step forward; others may not. The important piece of this activity is to generate data to process, to facilitate discussions on the distinctions between personal and group perceptions of the concepts of the "majority" and "minority." This will be addressed in the next step.]


FREE WRITING

» Ask students to return to their seats and write briefly about the exercise, considering the following questions.

What feelings did you experience?
Were you ever uncomfortable? When? Why?
Was it easier to be in the majority or minority?
Did other people's decisions about whether they felt like part of the minority or majority of a certain group surprise you? Why?
Did other people's decisions affect your own decisions?

If time permits, ask students to share their writings and perspectives with the class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES

» What kinds of personal and cultural statements have teenagers made in the wake of 9/11? Youth Outlook has a page of 9/11 coverage, and Youth Radio's "Generation 9-11" page is also devoted to the issue. Develop your own production—spoken word, multimedia presentation, story, or collage—to explain your culture, religion, or community to someone who you feel doesn't know or understand you. Email these presentations to info@sonicmemorial.org and we will post them to the site as examples for others.

Lesson 3: The Nation

The words "nationalism" and "patriotism" can mean very different things to different people.


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

How does nationalism differ from personal identity?
What does it mean to be a member of a society?
What are your rights and responsibilities as a member of this nation's unique culture?

Ask students to investigate the following media resources as they reflect on the questions above, in small group discussions or in writing exercises.


FREE WRITING

» In March 2002, Granta magazine published "What We Think About America," a collection of essays by 24 writers from many countries describing the part America has played in their lives—for better or worse—and delivering their estimates of the good and the bad it has done as the world's supreme political, military, economic, and cultural power. Have students read selections from this collection and ask them to consider the following questions: Is the United States really so disliked? Why? Have students write their own essay, "What I Think About America." If time allows, edit the works and "publish" your own class edition.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES

» Peter Ford is a staff writer for the Christian Science Monitor. His article "Why Do They Hate Us?" was published on September 27. In it, he reflects on a question asked in speech by President Bush and offers answers to that question from an Islamic perspective. After students read this article, have them write a letter to the editor in response to Ford's article.

NPR's This American Life presented the program "Before and After, Episode 194" on September 21, featuring a multitude of voices talking about September 11. In Act 5 of the program, Chicago Public Radio reporter Shirley Jahad describes white Chicagoans and Arab-American Chicagoans facing off, each side waving American flags and shouting "U.S.A.!" She talks about how each group means very different things by these identical actions.

Read Barbara Kingsolver's article "A Pure, High, Note of Anguish" and ask students to write a response.


Additional Resources

» Compare 9/11 to the attack on Pearl Harbor from December 7, 1941. (See the websites listed in the Civic Ideals and Practices unit for possible sources of information.) How have the definitions of "patriotism" and America changed?

» American RadioWorks, a documentary project of Minnesota Public Radio and NPR News, produces long-form documentaries for public radio. Its recent collaboration with the Center for Documentary Studies at Duke University, "Days of Infamy," focuses on man-on-the-street perspectives collected shortly after September 11, 2001, and the bombing of Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941.

» Densho is an organization that preserves primary source materials related to the World War II incarceration of Japanese Americans. The site contains questions for discussion about the events that led to the incarceration of 110,000 American citizens of Japanese descent and asks for a comparison to other events in history. Discuss the cultural perceptions that led to this internment of an entire ethnic group. How are things different today? How are they the same?


SUGGESTED LISTENING
WNYC/New York's website, Six Months: Rebuilding Our City, Rebuilding Ourselves, contains audio clips, including a reflection of Arab Americans discussing "What Is American?"

Ask students to listen to this segment and reflect on what it means to them to be American. Do they share the perspectives of the interview? Have the events of September 11 challenged their views of the United States?
