History and Time



This series of lessons is designed to help students identify important events and place them along a specific continuum—in this case, the time span of the World Trade Center's existence. It also creates a forum for discussing how individual memory plays a role in the creation of history. We each bring perspectives and values to events and artifacts. These perspectives help us define how and why we remember, causing the event, place, or person to take on a new identity in memoriam. The Sonic Memorial Project examines individual stories as a part of the historical record.

Learning outcomes:
Students will:   Identify important events in a specific time period
Identify and discuss what constitutes a historical event
Analyze different historical artifacts and categorize their importance





Lesson 1: Time Capsules



Time capsules have long been a popular way to send a message from a certain era into the future. When construction began on the WTC, work crews excavated a time capsule from the turn of the century. The New York Times created a website for the millennium to collect people's ideas for a national time capsule.

In the exercise that follows, the time capsule can be theoretical or actual. Either way, it should lead to discoveries about its creators: Each item chosen for inclusion stands for something, and most often, these representations are stories waiting to be told.


KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:

What do you consider your most valuable possession? Why?
What does this object say about you?
What was your most valuable possession five years ago, and what do you think it said about the person you were then?
How do values differ from person to person?
Can everyday objects document history? Why or why not?
What are other ways to document a time, a place, a person?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


BRAINSTORM

» Ask students to define the word "artifact" and to brainstorm artifacts that they think represent "history." Write students' ideas about artifacts on the board.


GALLERY DISCUSSION

» Engage the class in a "gallery" discussion: Post four pieces of paper around the room and have students line up so they can move from one piece to the other. At the first station, ask them to write down artifacts for a time capsule that represents their class.

At the second station, they should select artifacts that represent all students in their grade. At the third station, the artifacts represent the United States, and at the fourth, the world in the year 2002. Note: This exercise can be easily adapted to other groupings.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Concentrating on each of the four time capsules in turn, ask students to describe the artifacts they placed on the list and talk about the qualities that led them to choose those items.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES:

» Select five items from home that represent who you are. Be prepared to present your time capsule to the class and explain your criteria for the artifacts you chose. What did you consider bringing but decide not to include? Why? How would your time capsule be different if it had been created five years ago? Last week?

» Define five artifacts to be placed in a World Trade Center time capsule. Use the artifact criteria as your guide.

» As a class, vote on one place, artifact, or document that best represents your community to the world. Send a photograph or description and the rationale to us at info@sonicmemorial.org . We will post it alongside places from other communities nationwide.

Lesson 2: Timelines




KEY QUESTIONS

» These key questions can be used to introduce the lesson:
How does an event become a part of history?
How does an individual become a part of history?
What are the differences between personal history and national history?
How did the events of 9/11 make ordinary events and ordinary people a part of history?
What do you think are the five most important events of the last 40 years?


JOURNAL WRITING

» Briefly discuss these questions and have students write responses in their journals.


CLASS DISCUSSION

» Draw a line across a piece of chart paper, a white board, or the chalkboard. Write 1960 at one end of the line and 2002 at the other. Ask students to share the events they noted in their journals and give approximate dates for those events. Record their responses on the timeline.

Ask students whether they think all the events listed constitute "history." From this discussion, generate three to four criteria that make an event historical.

Focus the discussion on the World Trade Center. Visit the Sonic Memorial or the WTC timeline pages of sites such as InfoPlease.com and the Museum of the City of New York to find specific events and dates in the history of the buildings.

Ask students to create their own World Trade Center timeline. Alternatively, if class time is limited, you can prepare one in advance to hand out.


CLOSING THE LESSON

» To close the lesson, ask students to write the following on a notecard:

- three criteria for making an event historical
- two events or people that they found interesting
- the event or person that they would most like to know more about

Allow five to ten minutes for this reflection, then collect the cards. Review and return them with comments at the next class.


FOLLOW-UP ACTIVITIES:

» Listen to one of the full segments from the Sonic Memorial story features and write a biography about a person from that story. See if you can find out more about the person by researching at SonicMemorial.org or on the web.

» Create a personal/community timeline and include events that are historical to you and your community. Are the criteria for including an event on your timeline similar to or different from the class criteria? In what ways?

» Create an audio timeline of the WTC by linking to sounds in the Sonic Memorial archive. Be prepared to explain your selection of audio clips. Create a web page of your timeline.


Additional Resources



» Either of these lessons would serve as excellent preparation for doing fieldwork in the community, interviewing each other and members of the community, or creating a personal audio time capsule. Audio recording can be a rewarding and easy way to tell a story, and we'd love to help you succeed. For technical tips and other advice, see the "Teen Reporter's Handbook" on the Radio Diaries website.

» The National Park Service's "Teaching with Historic Places" is a web-based curriculum to enliven history, social studies, geography, civics, and other subjects by using properties listed in the National Register of Historic Places. Lesson plans are searchable by theme, location, time period, or U.S. History Standards.

» City Lore produces programs and publications about New York City's rich cultural heritage. The website provides instructional resources, publications, and links to many cultural, ethnographic, folkloric, and social history resources.
